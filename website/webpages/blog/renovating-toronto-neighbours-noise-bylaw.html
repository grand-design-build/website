Renovating in Toronto? Here's How to Keep Your Neighbours On Your Side

What Toronto's noise bylaw actually allows, and the handful of habits that keep a renovation from turning into a bylaw complaint.

Renovations are loud. Your neighbours hear it whether they want to or not, and a project that runs eight or ten weeks is a long time for someone next door to put up with drilling and dust.

You can't eliminate the noise. But you can control when it happens and how much warning people get, and that's what actually matters to the person living next to it.

What the Bylaw Actually Allows

Toronto's Municipal Code, Chapter 591, sets the hours construction noise is allowed. Outside of them, it isn't — permit or no permit.

WhenWhat's Allowed
Weekdays7:00 a.m. – 7:00 p.m.
Saturdays9:00 a.m. – 7:00 p.m.
Sundays & statutory holidaysNo construction noise permitted
Outside these hoursRequires a City-issued noise exemption permit

A neighbour who calls 311 about 8 p.m. drilling is reporting a bylaw violation, not being difficult. Repeated violations bring Municipal Licensing and Standards officers to the site — that's a bigger disruption to your timeline than the noise ever was.

The Three Things That Actually Prevent Complaints

  • Tell them before you start: A short note through the mail slot — what's happening, roughly how long, and who to call — does more than any amount of politeness mid-project. Most complaints come from surprise, not noise.
  • Keep the loudest work inside the loudest hours: Demo, concrete work, and anything with a compressor should land mid-morning to mid-afternoon on a weekday whenever the schedule allows — not because it's required, but because that's when the neighbourhood already expects some noise.
  • Give them a name and a number: "Call the site supervisor" is a better first stop than "call 311." A neighbour who can reach someone directly rarely needs to reach the City.

Parking and Driveway Access

Noise isn't the only friction point. A dumpster on the street, a crew truck blocking a driveway, or three trades' worth of vehicles taking up the block's parking causes just as much tension — and it lasts the whole project, not just the loud days.

Confirm with your contractor where the dumpster goes, where the crew parks, and whether any permits are needed for street occupancy. If a neighbour's driveway or usual spot gets blocked even once, that's the moment goodwill starts to erode. A five-minute conversation before the dumpster arrives prevents most of it.

What to Do If a Neighbour Complains Anyway

Even a fully permitted, well-run project can still draw a complaint. When it happens, the response matters more than the compliance.

Don't lead with "we're within our rights." That's true, but it's not what the conversation needs. Acknowledge the disruption, apologize for the specific thing that bothered them, and ask what would help. If the loud work can shift an hour later or the dumpster can move six feet, do it. Small, visible adjustments cost you little and buy back most of the goodwill.

Getting defensive, even when you're legally in the clear, tends to escalate. A neighbour who feels dismissed is more likely to start documenting and calling 311 regularly. One who feels heard usually lets it go after the first conversation.

What This Has to Do With Your Contractor

Scheduling, sequencing, and communication with the people next door are part of project management, not extras. The same discipline that keeps your renovation on budget also keeps it from turning into a bylaw complaint. A crew that shows up on time, sticks to its hours, and warns neighbours before the loud stuff starts is just running the job properly.
